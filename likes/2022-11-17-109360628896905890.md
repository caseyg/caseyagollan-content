    html: "<p>I'm curious to see how the politics of moderation and federation decisions play out here over the next year or so, assuming a significant fraction of the folks who have/are joining stick around.</p><p>I'm already looking at the instance where I have an alt deciding to defederate a couple of major server for (quite likely real) shortcomings in moderation — meaning I need to decide whether or not I migrate that account.  Either way, I likely won't be able to talk with both local friends and folks on those servers, as they'll eventually defederate with instances that don't follow suit.</p><p>The Mastodon moderation structure is vulnerable to mob rule, where if enough instance-managers decide someone should be burned out of the ecosystem, they will be, regardless of any attempt at an objective standard.  This isn't to say that the way companies handle moderation is good — it's still wildly underfunded at best, and their other goals (virality, ad revenue) work at direct cross-purposes to it.  It is, however, to say that these problems are hard and that Mastodon hasn't solved them any better than Twitter or FB have.  Not optimizing for virality or global reach buys a lot of space, but it's not a silver bullet.  Things like adequately funding moderator staff at scale across languages and timezones, structured mechanisms for sharing abuse information across instances without magnifying it, and binding oversight and transparency structures aren't on the table yet.</p><p>My friend Quinn wrote a long and useful thread about this over on twitter that I think folks here should consider: <a href=\"https://twitter.com/quinnnorton/status/1592569143433515008\" rel=\"nofollow noopener\"><span class=\"invisible\">https://</span><span class=\"ellipsis\">twitter.com/quinnnorton/status</span><span class=\"invisible\">/1592569143433515008</span></a>. I'd recommend reading it especially if you've found yourself disagreeing with some of her past work, for exactly that reason.</p>"
